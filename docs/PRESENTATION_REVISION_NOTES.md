# Writing overviews and research notes in Aw

Use this guidance for reader-facing writing in any Aw project. It applies to theoretical, empirical, methodological and expository work. Overviews and research notes have different levels of detail, but share the principles below. These are practical writing checks, not a new permissions policy or evidence that a claim has been verified.

## Start with the reader's question

Identify the intended reader and what they can reasonably be assumed to know. State the problem, why it matters and what the work aims to establish before introducing technical machinery. Use the human's example of a good explanation as a stylistic starting point. Definitions and analogies should clarify the argument without changing its meaning or scope.

When extending a familiar theory, begin with the problem the reader wants to solve, then introduce the established guarantee. Calling a theorem familiar or announcing what it means is not a natural introduction by itself. Show how the established method obtains that guarantee, then identify the choice or assumption the research changes. Explain why the resulting alternative is useful or surprising. Do not open with a broad class of methods or a taxonomy when a concrete classical example supplies the natural entry point. Keep a fitting objective distinct from the optimality criterion it attains, and distinguish finite-sample guarantees from asymptotic attainment.

Introduce an idea by its purpose before its formal representation: what question it answers, what the objects represent and why the proposed operation is needed. Then supply definitions, notation and the formal statement. A formula, diagram or standard term is not self-explanatory merely because specialists recognize it. Explain the interpretation after presenting it.

Name concepts by their meaning or role, not by internal working labels. Define unfamiliar terms when they first become necessary. Introduce symbols before using them, distinguish similar-looking objects, and make changes in a symbol's meaning explicit. State the reference point for comparisons and the context of qualified claims: better than what, conditional on what, or accurate according to which criterion? The manuscript must contain the context needed to understand it.

Manage notation over the whole argument, not just at first occurrence. Distinguish a fixed true or reference value from a candidate parameter and a dummy optimization or integration variable. When a symbol leaves an optimization display and becomes a free argument, state its role in the surrounding prose. Introduce an auxiliary symbol close to its first actual use. When an object returns after a substantial derivation, section break or change of viewpoint, briefly recall its meaning and the defining formula needed for the next step. A reference helps locate a definition but does not replace the local reminder when understanding the current calculation depends on it. Prefer role-revealing notation, such as a dotted parameter for its first variation, to a new generic letter the reader must memorize. Do not repeat every definition mechanically; restore the context that a reader could reasonably have lost.

Use a descriptive title that names the research problem or contribution. Avoid generic evaluative adjectives and slogan-like pairings that could label many unrelated projects.

Prefer conventional, direct section headings such as Introduction, Model, Proof, Numerical results and Conclusion, adding a specific subject where useful. Avoid rhetorical question-and-answer headings and labels such as “convention” when the section simply defines a standard object. Do not give a repeated contribution summary its own section merely to announce what the introduction already explains.

Never refer to a chat or conversation in manuscript prose. This includes indirect reassurance such as “the reader need not have followed the chat,” references to prompts or assistant turns, and explanations that depend on what was said earlier outside the document. State the scientific question and argument directly. Describe prior results by their content or a named source, and link saved evidence where useful. The manuscript is an independent explanation, not a narrated record of the interaction that produced it.

## Choose the right level of detail

An overview answers: what is the problem, what is the central idea, what has been established, and why does it matter? Aim for a few paragraphs or about a page, with a figure only if it helps and links to the detailed treatment. It is not a condensed log of every approach. Include technical detail only when it is essential to understanding the contribution. Introduce a constructed or project-specific object through its inputs, operations and output before giving it a short name. If explaining a secondary example requires several new objects or nested interpretations, keep the overview to its construction and purpose, then develop the metric or mechanism in the research note. Supporting derivations, protocols, secondary examples and administrative bookkeeping belong elsewhere.

Close an overview with the result's consequence and the next scientific question, not an abbreviated methods section. Keep optimizer names, approximation diagnostics and individual failure-case numbers in the detailed note unless they are themselves the overview's central finding.

Establish the motivating bottleneck and relevant prior approach before introducing the proposed construction. Explain what that approach holds fixed and what new choice the research explores. In a methodological overview, distinguish the object being designed from the criterion used to evaluate it: lead with why the design choice matters, then introduce its loss or guarantee. A precise description of a loss is not a substitute for motivation. When prior work supplies an intuition for a new proposal, distinguish that motivation from a benefit actually established for the proposal.

Give the overview a punchline: state what the work changes in our understanding or capabilities, relative to prior knowledge or an appropriate comparison, and under what conditions. A list of results is not a substitute for explaining their significance. When several components contribute to an outcome, explain their roles and attribute effects only as far as the evidence permits. Distinguish improvements in the result from reductions in the effort required to obtain it. Do not combine measurements from incompatible settings into a single attribution or universal headline.

A research note can develop the full argument: define the objects, state assumptions, explain the reasoning or method, present supporting evidence and identify unresolved questions. Explain how each section follows from the preceding argument. Preserve unsuccessful cases and reproducibility information in linked evidence or appropriately labelled sections. An overview can summarize their importance without reproducing every detail.

Organize the detailed text around the argument, not the chronology of discovery. Open each section with the question it answers and how it advances the whole. Give each paragraph a recognizable job, and make transitions explain why the next idea is needed. Keep essential qualifications close to the claims they constrain. Place version names, long filenames and administrative status where they support verification rather than interrupt the explanation.

In Aw, put authorized longer-form explanations in a manuscript notes root, rather than a sequence of oversized notebook summaries. Confirm the intended root and its current purpose first; an overview is an explicitly chosen short companion, not a replacement for the detailed note. Keep a short owning-notebook cell linking to both manuscripts and the exact evidence. Use ordinary independent TeX roots or preserve an existing parent/section structure within the user's scope. Creating a note does not authorize restructuring another manuscript, and switching the main root affects the whole project, not just one window.

## Make claims precise and explain their significance

A concise statement of the result can be clearer than a compressed description of how it was obtained. A lower bound does not by itself identify an attainable optimum or an estimator achieving it. State the admissible class and local or global scope. Define equality cases directly, rather than describing them through evaluative language, and distinguish limiting-distribution claims from convergence of moments. State the setting, assumptions, conclusion and relevant dependencies. For an empirical finding, identify what was studied, how it was assessed and the uncertainty or limitations. Explain what the result establishes beyond the starting point, where it is useful and where it does not apply. Put supporting detail at the level appropriate to the document.

Choose deliberately between a formula and a verbal summary in an overview. If the formula is central, display it and define its variables, fixed inputs and consequence. Link the construction to the detailed note instead of compressing several operations into a dense verbal formula. State the result directly; a comparison with an unstated weaker result usually obscures its value. Check dimension-dependent rates and distinguish a convex weight problem from discrete support selection before adopting suggested shorthand.

Make the logical structure visible, rather than merely adding more explanations around an uninterrupted derivation. The narrative should tell the reader what question is being answered, why the next result is needed and how it connects to the main goal. A named statement should isolate the claim being established. Its proof should contain the manipulations that establish it. Afterward, explain what has become available for the next step. This question–statement–proof–consequence pattern is especially useful in research notes, not just publication manuscripts. The adaptive permanent research note provides a concrete local example: its continuation-error lemmas feed a moment theorem, followed by a finite-work transfer, with the role of each link explained in prose.

Choose the environment by its logical role. Use a lemma for a supporting identity or estimate, a proposition for a substantial intermediate or methodological result, a theorem for the main guarantee, and a corollary for a consequence obtained from earlier results. Keep definitions and motivation outside proofs when they are needed to understand the statement. State the hypotheses and conclusion together before deriving them, then place the proof immediately afterward or give an exact forward reference. Do not leave a central claim implicit until the last line of its calculation. Conversely, do not turn every routine equality into a separate result or imply that a familiar identity is novel merely by naming it.

For a multi-stage argument, give a short dependency guide naming the results and their jobs. Within a long proof, signpost the major steps and the result each step uses. Distinguish a formal or conditional calculation from the theorem that justifies its assumptions or remainder. A proof sketch must be labelled as such. A structural revision should separate and organize the existing argument, not silently strengthen its claims. In Aw, check the assembled statement numbering, references and proof ownership after introducing or moving environments: an unlabelled proof can otherwise attach to the wrong preceding statement. Keep stable passage identities and preserve comments while changing these boundaries.

Distinguish three attribution levels. A result may already be established in the subject and setting being studied; a classical tool may be used in a potentially new application; or the work may establish an additional bound, extension or algorithmic consequence. Cite the nearest relevant original source beside the claim and say what that source actually supplies. Name the additional application or conclusion explicitly, with its assumptions, rather than labelling all classical machinery either novel or irrelevant to novelty. Correctness, priority and usefulness are separate questions: a complete proof does not establish first discovery, and use of a classical technique does not rule out a substantial contribution. Where priority is unverified, identify the proposed contribution without declaring it literature-certified. Explain what each such contribution enables relative to the established starting point.

Keep conclusions attached to the assumptions and evidence that support them. A result for an idealized model does not automatically apply to an implementation, a different population or a changed procedure. Distinguish definitions, established results, observations, interpretations and conjectures. Explain any additional argument needed to transfer a conclusion from one setting to another. A claim of novelty is separate from a claim of correctness or practical usefulness.

Use the human's suggested wording to understand the intended framing, while checking that its scope matches the evidence. Preserve direct, strong phrasing where supported; do not silently convert a limited comparison into a universal claim. Explain substantial departures from a suggested formulation briefly and concretely.

## Make the reasoning and method followable

When describing a method, explain its inputs, the operations in their actual order, its outputs and why each important step is needed. When developing an argument, identify what is assumed, what is inferred and what justifies the inference. Keep distinct mechanisms and their supporting arguments separate even when the overall method combines them.

Distinguish levels of description: the object being studied, its model or approximation, the procedure used to analyze it, and the actual measurement or implementation. Improving one level need not improve the others. State what an approximation preserves, what it changes and what error it introduces. For a proposed but unrealized method, explain the information or resources it would require and what has actually been carried out.

Explain potentially ambiguous technical language through the operation it describes. For example, a statement about concentration should identify what is concentrating and with respect to which law, rather than leave the reader to infer a sampling, geometric or probabilistic meaning. Once a design/evaluation distinction has been established, let later paragraphs advance the argument instead of repeatedly restating it.

Make the context of each expression explicit. A probability needs a specified source of randomness and any conditioning; an average needs a specified population or set of observations; an optimization needs a variable, fixed inputs, constraints and objective. These are applications of the same writing principle: tell the reader what the statement refers to and what is held fixed, rather than leaving them to reconstruct it.

Before introducing a correction, explain what would go wrong without it, what must be preserved and what is allowed to change. Distinguish choosing a design from solving an auxiliary problem for a fixed design. For multipliers, identify the constraints they enforce and explain why they are numbers or functions of particular variables. Define a normalization or centered subspace explicitly when removing a redundancy, and use that definition consistently. State whether solvability is being assumed, derived locally or proved globally; a formal stationarity equation alone does not establish existence.

Show the algebra that carries an argument. If a proof relies on cancellation, telescoping, a conditional expectation or a change of variables, write the relevant equality and explain its steps; saying that it happens is not a substitute. Use a proof environment for a proof, and keep the statement before its proof in both source and reading order. When an indexed family becomes a random variable, define that variable and at least one expectation as an explicit sum or integral. Introduce the algorithm's actual probability law separately from an ideal comparison law, including which choices the algorithm controls.

Keep cautions where the reader can understand their purpose. Introduce the main construction positively, then discuss a limitation alongside the result or example it qualifies. Remove repeated reminders of distinctions already made, especially negations responding to an unstated objection. Do not remove genuine hypotheses or adverse evidence. Use informative headings and explicit cross-references; avoid relying on an unresolved reference label or an unexplained internal nickname to carry the explanation. A recurring comment identifies a manuscript-wide writing pattern: check analogous later passages, not just the quoted sentence.

Attach evaluative words to explicit criteria. Explain what “accurate,” “efficient,” “robust,” “optimal” or “significant” means in the passage, the comparison being made, and how the criterion is established. Distinguish a rigorous guarantee from a measured estimate, and a convenient proxy from the outcome of real interest. State relevant costs, uncertainty and excluded conditions rather than relying on an impressive label.

Use examples to resolve a specific conceptual difficulty, and explain which general point they illustrate. Avoid defensive qualifications that answer no identifiable reader question. Retain limitations that change the interpretation of a claim, but remove repeated status disclaimers and irrelevant exclusions. Put administrative verification, review and literature-check reminders in anchored Aw comments or review tasks for human resolution; substantive uncertainty still belongs in the text.

Connect an example to its mechanism, not just its outcome. When two constructions differ, show how each produces its answer and which information or constraint accounts for the difference. State the target before describing an approximation to it. A calculation showing that a method fails is not yet an explanation of why it fails.

Name the concrete action or quantity in local claims: “this decision,” “the error” and “the improvement” need an identifiable referent. Work out a short illustrative calculation instead of leaving its conclusion as an assertion. For a recurrence, show the conditional quantity, the averaging step and the substitution linking it to the defined variables. Keep a useful classical name, but provide the derivation or a suitable reference. Prefer straightforward sentences to habitual semicolon or dash constructions; use punctuation to express the reasoning, not to manufacture a compressed, authoritative tone.

State logical relationships in complete sentences. Avoid routinely introducing an abstract label and a colon before the substantive explanation, as in “the key point is:” or “the requirement is:”. Say what the method requires, preserves or changes, and explain why. Reserve colons for actual lists or definitions where they improve readability; they should not supply the argument's structure.

Calibrate detail to the reader's actual uncertainty. Omit elementary restatements already explicit in the formula, but show the intermediate identity behind a non-obvious step. In a relative-entropy proof, identify the common reference measure, write each density ratio, and show which term integrates under which fixed marginal. “Expand,” “the cross term cancels” and “completing the square” should not hide the step on which the argument depends. Apply the lesson to analogous later proofs without mechanically expanding every routine calculation.

Separate a probabilistic interpretation from its coordinate algebra. Introduce the experiment and its states before calling a matrix a transition or an operator an averaging map. Then show how its entries encode that experiment. Give conservation, symmetry and a coordinate transformation separate explanatory steps when they serve different purposes. When reusing an earlier identity, name the identity and show the substitution or integration that yields the new formula, rather than expecting the reader to recognize it across several pages.

Choose notation that reveals the level of the object. Distinguish a finite-data estimate, an exact quantity conditional on those data, a general-measure object and a numerical approximation. Explain transitions between these levels and propagate a notation change through the affected formulas and implementation discussion. State the joint or sampling law before interpreting a conditional probability or expectation. Replace ambiguous phrases such as “quality reference” with the actual law, error or baseline being compared. Name an upper bound by the form of its bound, not the nonlinearity of the functional it controls, and remove comparisons with implausible unstated alternatives.

A comment identifies a failure in the reader's understanding, not a question to answer verbatim in the manuscript. Revise the surrounding explanation so the missing idea is introduced at the point where the argument needs it. Reorder definitions, examples or transitions when necessary. Do not append a conversational answer such as “the decision is…” to an otherwise disconnected paragraph. Read the revised passage together with its neighbors as if the comment had never existed: it should explain the subject naturally, with clear referents and a coherent progression. An anchored reply records how the concern was handled, but does not substitute for that contextual revision.

## Explain a figure's message

A reader-facing caption states the figure's question, what is shown, how to interpret its visual encoding and the main takeaway. Define unfamiliar quantities, units, symbols and comparisons. Include uncertainty or methodological qualifications that change the interpretation. For a conceptual diagram, explain the relationships or sequence it represents. A figure should help answer a reader's question, not merely decorate the document or reproduce a log.

Keep complete protocols, versions and provenance in the evidence record, with enough context in the manuscript to interpret the result. Include substantial contrary evidence when it changes the central claim; otherwise give a proportionate limitation and point to the full account. Never present selected successes as a general guarantee or discard inconvenient evidence to simplify the narrative.

## Report experiments as comparisons, not selected anecdotes

Report substantive experiments in the detailed **shared Aw research note**, not
only in private notebook outputs or a short overview. The note should explain the
protocol and results with complete relevant comparisons and readable tables/plots,
including important adverse cases. Keep raw data, code and run provenance in the
owning notebook and link them; sharing a note does not require exposing every
private record. When publication is requested, verify the deployed note/PDF is
the intended current version, not an older frozen export.

When substantial saved studies exist, present the complete relevant comparison matrix, including regressions, missing runs and unsuccessful estimators. Separate versions, screened/reused inputs and fresh confirmations; do not pool overlapping cohorts into an inflated experiment count. A short overview can state the main takeaway and link to these details.

Define each metric, its units, direction and cost boundary before showing tables. Distinguish actual error against an independent answer from estimated variability, agreement between methods and theoretical guarantees. Charge preparation where it changes the practical ranking, report sample counts/replications, and state whether counts or stopping rules were fixed before outcomes. Repeated returns of an exact calculation are not independent stochastic samples. A zero-only failed estimator is not a zero-variance winner.

Use bold for the stated numerical winner and a clearly defined symbol for the proposed method, with ties based on unrounded values. Verify that mathematical entries are actually bold in the PDF. Explain unavailable entries and warnings in captions. A lowest observed value is not automatically a statistically established ranking or an accurate result, especially when a few contributions dominate or the reference answer is unknown.

Plots should reveal patterns that a large table makes hard to see, such as ratios across settings, preparation versus sampling cost, or component gains versus overhead. Generate them from the same checked records as the tables; retain code, hashes and exact values in the owning notebook. Reanalysis is not a new experiment. Do not rerun studies merely to draw figures, and do not change a method in response to a reporting pass without a separately labelled validation.

When a related Aw project is suggested as a methodological reference, inspect its current saved algorithm and evidence, not just an older manuscript or familiar baseline. Explain what the candidate method changes and how to implement that step. Keep its performance in the reference problem separate from performance on the present objective. A promising optimizer belongs in a clearly labelled next comparison until that comparison has actually run.

## Check the view the reader uses

PDF compilation and Aw's rendered reading view are different checks. A PDF can contain a correct figure while the reading view shows an unsupported source command. Verify a reported display problem in its actual view rather than substituting a successful compile.

Aw now displays literal project-local `\includegraphics` references to PNG, JPEG, GIF and WebP files in the reading preview, with figure captions and simple widths. PDF figures have an explicit open link; custom graphics macros, `\graphicspath`, SVG/EPS and precise float layout still require the LaTeX PDF. Use explicit project-relative paths such as `figures/comparison.png`. Missing or unsupported assets should remain visible as a diagnostic, not disappear. Imported chat images remain blocked; manuscript image access is scoped to its project catalogue and the guarded file endpoint.

Use a focused isolated browser trial for app changes. For manuscript revisions, inspect the changed reading passages and the relevant figure, without rerunning scientific experiments or conducting an exhaustive appearance sweep. Never clear browser recovery storage just to obtain a cleaner check: it may contain unfinished human drafts.

## Preserve review and research history

Read the current saved comments and owning manuscript purpose before editing. Preserve stable passage IDs where practical, original comment quotes, source snapshots, notebook outputs and unrelated prose. During an authorized revision pass, an agent may resolve a straightforward comment after verifying that its entire request is addressed in the saved text. Leave comments open when they involve substantive assessment, ambiguous intent, incomplete changes or a choice the human should make. A proposal awaiting approval is not a completed fix.

Treat intervening source edits as new evidence, not a reason to force a stale completion. Re-read the changed passage, preserve the human's intent and verify the saved version before resolving comments. If content is unexpectedly duplicated or replaced, inspect block identity as well as prose. Explicit passage markers must be unique, and generated position IDs must not alias them after insertion or splitting. Fix and regression-test an editor identity failure before continuing to use the affected editing workflow. Do not assume an unexplained deletion was intentional.

Add an anchored assistant reply when it helps explain what changed, why a request was handled differently, what was checked or what remains unresolved. Add a new comment for a distinct concern, rather than hiding it in an unrelated reply. Replies should describe the actual attempt, not presume the objection is correct or claim checks that were not performed. A simple fix needs only a brief completion record, not another long discussion. Report which comments were resolved and which remain for assessment.

Resolution closes a particular issue; it does not approve publication wording, certify a scientific claim or unlock settled text. Never delete the review history or resolve all comments merely because a writing pass is finished. Use explicit comment IDs and revision-guarded saves. Dedicated comment-only review requests still return comments without editing or resolving; follow their narrower scope.

Keep one short current-state notebook cell linked to the full note and saved evidence. Record editorial changes as editorial changes, not new experiments or proof verification. Reuse saved results and their provenance. Version substantial phases rather than rebuilding old evidence or silently moving notebook owners. Record reusable lessons here when they change how another agent should work; do not turn every small edit into another mandatory checklist.
